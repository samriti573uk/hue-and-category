    Did you know?

    Changing only the colour palette of a logo altered how survey participants categorised the brand — the mark itself never changed.

    The evidence

    Why the tool recommends what it recommends.

    A 71-person survey compared real colour pairings on two fictional logos. The patterns below are what the recommendation engine is built on.

    71participants compared paired logo versions across colour palettes
    77%preferred a red-and-yellow palette over pink and purple for a food brand
    76%said changing the palette alone changed how they perceived the brand

    Head-to-head palette preference

    For six paired questions, participants picked one palette over a named alternative — a red-and-yellow food logo against pink-and-purple, a warm or cool tone for trust, and so on. Every bar below is the winning option's share of the vote.

    This is the evidence the recommendation engine draws on: a palette only outranks another here because participants actually chose it over a specific alternative.

    Existing brand audit

    Before the survey, real food and beverage brands were audited for their dominant colours. The pattern was consistent enough to shape the original hypothesis.

    McDonald's, Burger King, KFC and Pizza Hut all lead with red, yellow, or both — brand messages built around energy, appetite and excitement.
    Pepsi, Aquafina, Dasani and Evian all lead with blue — messages built around trust, freshness and purity.

    A separate 2024 study analysing dominant logo colours across 644 food and beverage companies found the same warm/cool split — independent evidence the pattern here isn't specific to this survey's eight brands.

    Two logos, two identities

    A fast-food logo ("Burgo") and a healthy-food logo ("Zesta") were each shown in two colourways. Burgo in red and yellow was read as fast food by 91.5% of participants; recoloured pink and purple, most guessed fashion instead (38%), and only 21% still said fast food.

    Zesta followed the same pattern: green and orange was preferred by 70.4% over black and gold, and rated the most appealing version in the entire survey (3.8 / 5). Black and gold pulled the same mark toward "premium" and "luxury" instead of "healthy."

    About this tool

    Methodology, limitations & references

    The detail behind the Insights page, for anyone who wants to check the working.

    How this shapes the tool

    Every palette offered in the generator is tagged against the brand categories it tested well for. The confidence score you see on the results page reflects how closely a palette matches the established pattern for your chosen category — not a random number.

    Method

    A literature review on colour psychology, an audit of existing food and beverage brand identities, and a 71-participant survey using two fictional logos ("Burgo" and "Zesta"), each shown in two colourways.

    Scope

    Findings are specific to food and beverage branding contexts. Two categories — Fast Food and Beverage / Healthy Food — were directly tested; the site doesn't extend its claims beyond that scope.

    Hypothesis

    H₁

    Warm colour palettes are more strongly associated with food brands, while cool colour palettes are more strongly associated with beverage brands. Changing a brand's colour palette may alter consumer perception, category recognition and purchase intention.

    H₀

    Changing a brand's colour palette does not significantly influence consumer perception, brand personality, or category recognition.

    Research design

    The survey ran for two weeks (20–28 June 2026) and asked 33 questions covering general colour associations, paired logo comparisons, and trust/purchase drivers. Fictional logos were used instead of real brands, so responses reflected colour itself rather than existing brand familiarity.

    Limitations

    Worth reading these findings with their edges in view: the survey tested two fictional logos in one industry, with a self-selected online sample of 71 people. It shows a clear, real pattern within that scope — it doesn't establish how far the same effect holds for other product categories, other cultures, or logos with more complex imagery than a wordmark.

    The "pattern match" confidence scores this site shows for untested categories are explicitly extrapolations, not survey results.

    Directly tested

    Fast Food and Beverage / Healthy Food, using two logos, four colourways total.

    Extrapolated

    Every other category and palette pairing on this site, built from the general colour-association data rather than a paired test.
